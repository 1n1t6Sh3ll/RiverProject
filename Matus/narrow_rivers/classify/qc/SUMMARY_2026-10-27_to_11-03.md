# What we did, 27 October – 3 November 2026

Nenana River, sub-pixel river mapping. The goal hasn't changed: hand-label Landsat (30 m)
pixels, let a Random Forest extend those labels across whole scenes, and aggregate the result
into per-cell fractions for VIIRS (375 m). Sentinel-2 (10 m) is only something we look at
while digitising and checking. It never goes into the model.

This covers the week since the first Random Forest runs. Most of it was spent working out
where the errors actually come from, and a fair amount of it was finding out that our first
explanation was wrong.

## Where we started

By 26 October we had a working Random Forest for two dates: 2024-06-22 (summer, river and
land) and 2023-10-04 (freeze-up, river, ice, snow and land). It's tested with 5-fold
cross-validation where whole polygons are held out, never individual pixels. Pixels inside one
hand-drawn polygon are near-copies of each other, so a pixel-level split would score ~99 % and
tell us nothing.

Two lessons from that first round still hold. Balanced class weights inflated October river
area from 11.9 to 37.9 km², so we turned them off. And terrain shadow in October was being
called river until two shadow polygons were drawn as land. Ice versus snow stayed poor (snow
recall 45 %), and that turned out to be a definition problem, not a labelling one.

Summer river recall was 84.5 % with 93 % precision. The question for the week was how to get
that higher.

## Checking the labels first

The first suspicion was that the labels themselves were wrong. A quick check suggested
658 river pixels (about 11 %) had no water in them. That check was flawed: it thresholded
Sentinel-2 NDWI at zero, and a Landsat pixel that's 30–50 % water never pushes NDWI above zero.
Done properly, the number is about 97 pixels, or 1.6 %. So the labels are fine, and fixing
them was never going to get us to 95 %.

Along the way we found that gravel islands fool NDWI badly. Two dry gravel islands
(polygons 39 and 20) read as 85 % water on a simple water/land mix. Unmixing against four
reference spectra (water, gravel, dark forest, vegetation) fixed it, because SWIR separates
gravel from water very cleanly. False "this land is water" flags dropped from 1,992 to 34.
NDWI and unmixing each fail on something different, gravel for one and dark vegetation for the
other, so we use the minimum of the two.

Two things got settled with the professor here. Polygon 89, a channel that looked dry on
every band and every summer date we have, is a real water channel visible on Sentinel-2 and
stays in as river. And we agreed a labelling rule: if any single Sentinel-2 pixel inside a
Landsat pixel has water in it, the whole Landsat pixel is water, because it's already a
partial pixel.

We also tried adding a separate "mixed" class for bank edges and narrow threads. The result
was inconclusive. Two reasonable Sentinel-2 definitions of partial water disagree by almost
a factor of two on total area, so there was no trustworthy reference to judge it against.
River recall also got slightly worse with the extra class, so we dropped it.

## Narrow channels are a resolution limit

Most of the remaining summer errors sit in a handful of polygons: five of 49 river polygons
hold about two thirds of all missed river pixels, and they're the narrow channels. Polygons
under 20 pixels are recalled only about 21 % of the time.

We tested whether giving the model neighbourhood context would help, things like the wettest
pixel nearby, distance to open water and the long-term JRC water record. It didn't. Recall
moved by less than a point in every variant. When a channel is narrower than a Landsat pixel,
its neighbours look dry too, so there's nothing for context to pick up. The water is real
(you can see it on Sentinel-2), but Landsat can't resolve it. We concluded that ~85 % is close
to the ceiling for those channels at 30 m.

## The cloud mask was hiding the river

The next step was the real test: train on June, then predict other summer dates the model has
never seen (2023-07-24, 2025-07-04 and 2026-08-01). The first numbers looked excellent, around
96–99 % recall on the main channel. Then you opened the 2023-07-24 map in QGIS and saw large
parts of the river simply missing.

The cause was Landsat's own cloud mask (QA_PIXEL). It flags silty summer water, and river
ice, as cloud or shadow. On 2023-07-24 it masked 77.7 % of the main channel, even though those
pixels are at river temperature (~298 K) and nowhere near as cold as real cloud tops. We had
been leaving masked pixels out of the scoring, so the good numbers only covered the part of
the river the mask let through. Counting everything, the real recall was 22–25 %.

We dropped the mask entirely. We check the scenes for cloud by eye, and no date has the very
cold pixels you'd expect from cloud tops. With the mask gone, main-channel recall on the
other dates went back up to 96–97 %, and this time it's honest.

## Anchoring: making one date comparable with another

Even without the mask, a model trained on June doesn't transfer to other dates as is. On
2025-07-04 it called 88–91 km² of river when the real figure is about 13 km².

The obvious guess was sun angle, but that's not it. 2025-07-04 has almost the same sun as
June (47.9° vs 48.6°) and the biggest shift of all. Surface reflectance is also already
corrected for sun angle on flat ground. What actually changes is this:

| date | sun | blue | green | SWIR1 | land temperature |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2024-06-22 (training) | 48.6° | 0.015 | 0.035 | 0.119 | 300.5 K |
| 2023-07-24 | 44.9° | 0.021 | 0.045 | 0.126 | 306.4 K |
| 2025-07-04 | 47.9° | 0.024 | 0.054 | 0.122 | 302.0 K |
| 2026-08-01 | 43.0° | 0.020 | 0.040 | 0.122 | 300.1 K |

*(median values over land that has never been wet)*

Blue and green on dry land are up to 55 % brighter than in June, while SWIR barely moves.
That's what leftover haze or smoke looks like (July is wildfire season), not a change in
illumination. Temperature varies with the weather, 6 K warmer on 2023-07-24.

The fix is simple. On every date, we take the median of each feature over land that the JRC
record says has never been wet, and subtract it. The model then learns "how different is this
pixel from ordinary dry land today" instead of absolute values, and the same is done when
predicting. With that, 2025-07-04 comes out at 14 km² instead of 91.

It isn't perfect. It removes a constant offset but not a scaling. It uses one correction for
the whole box, so patchy haze isn't fully handled. And on 2026-08-01 the un-anchored model
isn't clearly worse. So the claim is that anchoring fixes the hazy dates, not that it wins
everywhere. 2026-08-01 also has a separate problem: the main channel is only ~63 % recalled
there, and we haven't worked out yet whether that's silty water or the channel moving.

## More labels, in the right places

Looking at the new maps, you could see wide channels the model was missing simply because
nothing like them had ever been labelled. So we labelled more, on June only, keeping
2025-07-04 completely unlabelled as a test date.

**First round:** 14 land polygons, including several small islands, plus one wide river
channel (polygon 117). The land polygons turned out to teach the model nothing, because it was
already calling them land with almost full confidence. Polygon 117 was the useful one: the old
model missed 60 % of it.

**Second round:** four more wide channels like 117 (polygons 121–124), spread across the
map. This is the clearest result of the week. With one example of this kind of channel, the
model can't learn it. With several, they teach each other:

| held-out channel | original labels | + 117 | + 121–124 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 121 | 0.06 | 0.09 | **0.88** |
| 122 | 0.09 | 0.14 | **0.75** |
| 123 | 0.63 | 0.72 | **0.75** |
| 124 | 0.03 | 0.20 | 0.24 |
| 117 | 0.41 | 0.39 | 0.42 |

*(share of each channel the model finds when that channel is left out of training)*

This also corrects something we said earlier. More river labels don't help for narrow,
sub-pixel channels, but they clearly do help for wide channels that were just never labelled.

There's a cost. On the original polygons, river recall went from 84.5 % to 86.3 %, but
precision fell from 93.1 % to 89.5 %, and June river area grew from 12.4 to 15.0 km². About
three quarters of the extra area is where JRC has seen water before, and most of it is banks
getting slightly wider, which fits the "any wet Sentinel-2 pixel" rule. Roughly a fifth of it
is probably false. The worst false river is polygon 107, a patch of bright, hot, bare ground
that has never been wet. On the other summer dates the picture is steady: main-channel recall
is about the same, and the model finds 3–6 points more of the permanently wet channel.

The practical lesson for labelling is to label where the model is wrong, not where it's
already right. The next labels should be land: more bare, bright ground like 107, and gravel
islands the map currently calls river.

## How we measure accuracy, and what that leaves out

We measure accuracy in three ways:

1. **Cross-validation on our own labels** with whole polygons held out. This is the main number.
2. **Transfer to unseen dates**, scored on things that don't change between years: June land
   polygons, and June river pixels where JRC says there's water at least 80 % of the time.
3. **Sanity checks.** The implied river width (246 m) is within 5 % of the SWORD river
   database (234 m), the total area is reasonable, and predicted river mostly falls where JRC
   has seen water.

All three use pixels we chose to label, and people tend to label the clear cases, so these
numbers are probably a bit optimistic. The unbiased number would come from the blind audit:
200 random points the model has already classified, which we'd label without seeing its
answer. We haven't done it yet. It's worth doing before anything goes into a paper.

## Where things stand

| | river recall | river precision | land recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| June, cross-validation, current labels | 80.7 % | 86.0 % | 98.0 % |
| June, original polygons only | 86.3 % | 89.5 % | 98.4 % |
| 2023-07-24, main channel (unseen date) | 97.2 % | – | 95.6 % |
| 2025-07-04, main channel (test date) | 95.1 % | – | 95.8 % |
| 2026-08-01, main channel | 63.7 % | – | 97.3 % |

The overall June figure dropped mainly because the new wide channels are harder cases than
the old polygons, not because the model got worse.

For showing the professor how this works on a date it was never trained on, we built the
full set of viewing layers for 2023-07-24: true colour, colour infrared, SWIR, NDWI and MNDWI
from Landsat, plus the same-day Sentinel-2 scene taken 30 minutes later. The zoomed figure
makes the current weakness easy to see. The model gets the main channel and most threads,
but misses several braid threads that are obvious on Sentinel-2. On that date the silty water
sits just below zero on NDWI, so it looks a lot like land.

**Still open:**
